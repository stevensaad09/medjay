How to Use This Guide

This is Day 5, the last day of Retail Sales Essentials. You work alone, at your own pace. Read a section, then stop and do the short reflection before moving on. Your words will not match the examples, and they shouldn't. Keep the order of the steps and let the words sound like you. Write your answers down rather than thinking them through. Writing forces you to commit to a sentence, and a sentence you can say out loud is the only kind that helps you at a counter. Two ideas from earlier days run through everything here. The first is that customers decide mostly on feeling, quickly, and then justify the choice afterward. The second is that pressure creates resistance, so a close built on evidence and a real choice beats one built on urgency. Today you learn how to finish well, and how to make the finish the beginning of a relationship.

The examples in this guide use fictional names, products and numbers. Use them as shapes to copy, not as facts about any real store.

Today's Objectives (KASU)

Knowledge
You will know the 5 steps of the Commitment Close and the 5 conditions to check before you ask for any commitment.
Attitude
You will treat the close as a service to the customer rather than a favor you are asking for, and treat after-sale care as part of the sale, not an extra.
Skill
You will be able to close a counter sale using a calibrated how or what question, or a genuine choice with a date, and practice it whether or not you have a retail job yet.
Upskill
You will be able to handle one after-sale problem calmly and turn it into a reason for the customer to come back.

Why Closing Feels Hard, and Why It Shouldn't

Most new retail staff freeze at the close. The customer has said the product looks good, the conversation is going well, and then the moment arrives and the salesperson says something like "So, do you want to take it?" The customer hesitates, says they will think about it, and walks out. Look at what happened. The question asked for a yes or no. A yes/no question at the end of a good conversation puts the customer in a corner. Saying yes feels like a commitment they are not sure about yet. Saying no feels rude after you have been helpful. So they take the third option: they leave and think about it. That is not indecision. It is a polite escape from an uncomfortable question. There is a second reason the close feels hard. Retail staff often feel they are asking the customer for something. The truth is the opposite. A customer who came in and found the right product wants to finish. The unfinished decision sits in their mind. A clear, honest close gives them permission to complete what they started. When you do it well, the customer feels relief, not pressure.

One more thing from Day 1: the customer has usually decided emotionally before you get to the close. Your job at the close is not to persuade. It is to make the decision feel safe, clear and final.

That means restating what they told you, showing the evidence plainly, being honest about what happens if they wait, and then offering a real next step.

The Commitment Close

The Commitment Close (MEDJAY adaptation, based on Voss, Kahneman, Dixon and Adamson, and Cialdini) is a close built on the customer's own words. It is the logical conclusion of the conversation, not a push. It has 5 steps. The customer can still say no at every one of them, and that is what makes it work.

Step 1, Criteria: Restate the customer's own criteria, in their words. Not what you think they need. What they said. "You told me it has to fit in a small space, be easy to clean, and last more than a couple of years."

Step 2, Evidence: Show the evidence against those criteria. Point to the product, the label, the demonstration, the review. "This one fits in the corner by your door, the surface wipes clean in one pass, and the warranty covers it for three years."

Step 3, Consequence: Name the consequences honestly, including when waiting is better. This is where most people flinch. They think honesty weakens the close. It does the opposite. If waiting is genuinely better for this customer, say so. If the size they need is out of stock until next week, say so. Customers can tell when they are being handled, and honesty is what makes the rest of your words believable.

Step 4, Calibrated question: Ask a how or what question. "What would you need to see to feel confident taking this one today?" A calibrated question asks the customer to think and answer in their own words. It also tells you what still stands in the way.

Step 5, Genuine choice: Offer real choices of next step, with a date. "Would morning pickup on Saturday or delivery on Monday suit you better?" Both options are real. The customer chooses, and the decision gets a date.

Before you run these 5 steps, check that all 5 of these are true:

  • The customer's criteria are clear and in their words.
  • The evidence is verified and shared.
  • The consequences of each option, including waiting, are honest.
  • The decision-maker is present or included.
  • The next step is specific, proportionate and dated.

If one is missing, your job is to fix that, not to close. Ask about the person they need to check with. Go and verify the delivery date. If the criteria are still vague, go back to questions. A close attempted before these are true is the reason so many new staff hear "I'll think about it."

Why does the Commitment Close work on the customer's mind? Three reasons. First, the customer hears their own words coming back, which builds trust and keeps them from feeling sold to. Second, they are comparing the evidence to criteria they set themselves, which feels like their decision, because it is. Third, a real choice with a date removes the "maybe later" escape without pressure, because both options are next steps and neither is a rejection.

After-Sale: The Psychology of Satisfaction and Loyalty

Here is something worth knowing early. A customer's satisfaction after a purchase is not fixed. It is built afterward, from how the store treats them once the money has changed hands. Two customers can buy the same product and leave with different feelings about your store, depending on what happens next.

Customers re-run the decision in their mind after buying. This is normal and human. They look for evidence they chose well. If the first thing that happens is a problem and someone solves it quickly, the re-run ends with "good choice, and they took care of me." If the first thing is a problem and nobody answers, the re-run ends with "I should have gone elsewhere." Your after-sale care is shaping which story they tell.

Repeat customers come from a few plain behaviors.

Give a clear handover. Explain how to use it, what to do if something goes wrong, and how to reach the store. Confusion after purchase reads as a warning sign.

Keep one small promise exactly. Say you will call when the replacement part arrives, and call. A kept small promise does more for trust than a large one, because it is checkable and it happens soon. Remember them next time. When a customer returns, greet them as someone you have met. If you noted what they bought, ask how it is going. This is not a gimmick. It signals that they matter beyond the transaction.

Make it easy to come back. A customer with a problem should not have to fight to be helped. Handle what you can on the spot, and tell them plainly what happens next if it needs more.

There is a strong pull in any store to get a problem off your counter, and that pull often leads to half-truths. Resist it. A customer who finds out later that a defect was known and not mentioned becomes a lost customer and a source of bad word of mouth. An honest answer about a limitation now keeps the relationship, and the customer usually respects it.

When the Customer Says No at the Close

A no at the close is information, not rejection. Use A.R.T. from Day 3: Acknowledge, Reframe, Transition. Use it only for objections and concerns, and always as one chain: name the concern with no "but" after it, shift the decision from the visible cost to what happens if they wait, then ask a how or what question.

Practice example: a customer looks at a 240-unit home coffee maker and says it is too expensive. The salesperson says, "You're wondering whether it's worth the extra." The customer nods. "The cheaper one you looked at needs replacing sooner, and you said you make coffee twice a day. What would make the difference clear for you?" The customer says, "If it lasts twice as long it pays for itself." The salesperson shows the warranty terms and asks which pickup day works.

If the customer wants to think about it, do not just let them drift. Set a real return point. "No problem. I'll hold the display model for you until Friday, and either day works if you come back." If a customer asks for a lower price or another concession, remember the rule: trade, don't concede. Ask for something of equal value in return, such as the display model at a small reduction, or taking two items today. A discount handed over for nothing teaches the customer to ask again.

Apply It in Your Role

However you are reading this guide, find a way to practice. If you work in a store, use your counter. If you don't, you can still do every task below.

If you work at a counter or till: keep the criteria summary short and spoken. Two or three points is enough. Then ask the calibrated question and offer two dated options.

If you work on a showroom floor: walk the customer back to the product while you restate their criteria. Point at the evidence as you say it, because seeing the thing being described makes it easier to believe.

If you work in a store with a returns desk: the after-sale moment often happens there. A customer bringing something back is still a customer. Solve what you can and tell them what happens next. This is the moment repeat business is won or lost.

If you work in a small shop where you know regulars by name: your advantage is memory. Greet returning customers as returning customers, and ask about the last purchase.

If you have no retail job yet: practice without one. Visit any store and watch how staff close and hand over a sale. Write down one thing they did at each of the 5 steps. Then role-play a full close with a friend, and record yourself on your phone so you can hear your own pace and wording. For every role, record one thing daily: how many customers you spoke to, and how many left with a dated next step, whether a purchase or a return visit. If you have no customers yet, record your practice conversations instead. That number, not your feeling about the day, is what shows you your progress.

What Would You Say?

Write your answer before reading the model answers at the end. Keep the steps, lose the script.

  1. A customer has praised the product for ten minutes and then goes quiet. What do you say to move to a close?
  2. A customer says "I'll think about it" with no other reason given. What is your next sentence?
  3. A customer calls two days after buying, annoyed that a part is missing from the box. What do you say first?
  4. You are about to close, but the customer mentions her partner has not seen the item. What do you do?
  5. A customer asks for a lower price "because I'm buying two." What do you say?

Note Watch for the instinct to answer the question for the customer in item 1. Give the quiet a second or two before you speak.

Key Takeaways

  • The close is a service. A customer who found the right product wants to finish the decision.
  • Yes/no questions at the close create polite escapes. Ask a how or what question, or offer a genuine choice with a date.
  • The Commitment Close runs on the customer's own words: Criteria, Evidence, Consequence, Calibrated question, Genuine choice.
  • Check the 5 conditions before you close. If one is missing, fix it rather than push.
  • Honesty about waiting is part of the close, not a weakness in it.
  • After-sale care shapes how the customer remembers the decision. Keep one small promise exactly.
  • A no at the close is information. Use A.R.T., MEDJAY's own framework, and let it end with a how or what question.
  • You can practice every one of these without a job, by watching other stores, role-playing with a friend, or recording yourself.

Quiz

Choose one answer for each question.

  1. Which sequence matches the Commitment Close?

    1. Criteria, Evidence, Consequence, Calibrated question, Genuine choice
    2. Acknowledge, Reframe, Transition, Close, Follow-up
    3. Anchor, Illuminate, Mobilize, Confirm, Close
    4. Open, Question, Present, Object, Close

  2. A customer says she wants to buy but needs her partner to see the item first. What should you do?

    1. Close now before she changes her mind
    2. Offer a small discount so she decides today
    3. Fix the missing condition: agree when the partner can see it, and set a day
    4. Tell her most customers decide on their own

  3. Why does a yes/no close often lead to "I'll think about it"?

    1. Customers do not like the product enough
    2. A yes/no question puts a customer who is unsure in a corner, so they take the polite escape
    3. Staff speak too slowly
    4. Products are usually out of stock

  4. A customer asks for a lower price because he is buying two items. What fits the rule on concessions?

    1. Give the discount, since two items are worth more
    2. Refuse and say the price is final
    3. Ask for something of equal value in return, such as the same-day purchase or the display model
    4. Offer the discount but say nothing about it

  5. Two days after buying, a customer calls about a missing part. Which first response builds repeat business?

    1. "That's the manufacturer's problem, not ours."
    2. Say nothing and hope it resolves itself
    3. Offer a discount on their next purchase instead of solving it
    4. Acknowledge the problem plainly, tell them exactly what happens next, and do it

Upskill Challenge

This is your practice for the next week. Do it with support from a colleague, or with a friend if you have no retail job yet.

  • Watch two full counter closes, in your own store or any store you visit. Write down one thing you saw for each of the 5 steps.
  • With a colleague present, or in a role-play or self-recording if you have no job yet, close one sale using the 5 steps. Afterward, note which step felt most awkward.
  • Make one small promise to a customer after a sale, or make one to a friend in a role-play. Write it down with the date, and keep it exactly.
  • When a customer says no or "I'll think about it," run A.R.T. once and note the how or what question you asked.
  • Start a simple record: conversations, dated next steps agreed, purchases made. Review it at the end of the week.
  • Ask a colleague, or a friend who watched you, for one piece of feedback on your after-sale handover, and use it on your next attempt.

Practice example: one week you speak to 40 customers and agree a dated next step with 16 of them; 9 of those 16 come back on the agreed day. Note your own numbers as they happen; do not fill these in with real sales you have not made.

Model Answers

These are examples, not scripts. Keep the order and the principle; use your own words.

  1. "You've looked at it carefully. You told me you wanted it to fit on the small table, be easy to clean, and last. It fits, the surface wipes clean, and the warranty covers it for three years. What would you need to see to feel good about taking this one today?" Why it works: it restates the customer's criteria, shows evidence, and asks a calibrated question instead of a yes/no.
  2. "Of course. What part of it still feels unclear?" Why it works: it does not chase and does not push. It gives the customer room to name the real hesitation, which is often something you can solve.
  3. "Thank you for calling, and I'm sorry the box was incomplete. Let me take the order number and tell you exactly what I'll do. Is it easier if you come by today or if we send the part to you?" Why it works: it acknowledges, tells them what happens next, and offers a real choice with a date.
  4. "That makes sense, it's a big decision. When could your partner come in? I can hold it until then, and either the evening or the weekend works." Why it works: the missing decision-maker is the condition to fix. A dated hold moves things forward without pressure.
  5. "I can look at what's possible for two. If we can adjust something on the price, would you be ready to take both today, or is the display model the better fit?" Why it works: it trades rather than concedes, and offers a genuine choice with a date. Two weak examples, for contrast. Weak: "So, shall we go ahead?" This is a yes/no close and invites the polite escape. Weak: "Would you like to sign up today?" Same problem, and it centers your outcome rather than the customer's decision.

Quiz answers:

  • 1. A. The 5 steps run in this order, from the customer's criteria to a dated choice.
  • 2. C. If a decision-maker is missing, you fix that before closing; a close without it usually ends in delay.
  • 3. B. A cornered customer escapes; a how or what question gives them a way forward.
  • 4. C. Trade, don't concede: a concession is exchanged for something of equal value, never handed over.
  • 5. D. After-sale honesty and follow-through are what the customer remembers, and what brings them back.
