How to Use This Guide

Today is about the first minute and the next ten. A customer walks in, you open the conversation, and what you find out in that time decides whether the rest of your sale works or stalls.

Work through this guide in order. It explains the idea, shows you an example, then asks you to reflect. Read the examples as examples, not as lines to memorize. Your words need to sound like you. A customer can hear a script from across the store.

Do the "What Would You Say?" exercises before you read the model answers at the end. Write your answer down first. The principle matters more than the exact wording.

You build on yesterday. On Day 1 you learned how customers decide: fast automatic System 1 thinking, slow deliberate System 2 thinking, emotional triggers, and how perceived value sits next to price. Keep that in mind all day. Today you learn how to open the conversation and find out what the customer actually needs.

Today's Objectives (KASU)

Knowledge
You will know the 4 steps of the First-Contact Standard and the 4 rungs of the Question Ladder, and why each one works on the customer's mind.
Attitude
You will shift from "I need to present something" to "I need to understand something." A good opening earns the right to keep talking. It doesn't demand it.
Skill
You will be able to open a conversation with a walk-in and climb the Question Ladder from situation to need, using open questions and listening more than you speak.
Upskill
You will run an opening and discovery conversation with a real customer, supervised, and review it afterward against the First-Contact Standard and the Question Ladder.

The First Steps of a First Contact

Think about the last time someone started a conversation with you and you wanted it to stop. Maybe a phone call, maybe someone at a counter. What did they do?

Most people answer the same way: they talked fast, they pushed, or they gave you no easy way out. In those first seconds, the customer's mind is asking one silent question before anything else: Is this safe?

This is System 1 doing its job. It scans for threat before it processes anything else. A fast approach, a too-loud voice, or a pitch that starts before a hello all register as pressure. Pressure creates resistance. That is a basic human reaction: when people feel pushed, they push back, even when the offer could help them.

The First-Contact Standard (MEDJAY adaptation, based on Kahneman, Brehm and Guyer et al.) gives you a four-behavior structure for every first contact, in this order: Low Threat, Autonomy, Relevance, Curiosity. It is a structure, not a script. Its goal is an opening the other person chooses to continue: one that earns attention instead of demanding it.

Low Threat: Open calmly and unhurried. Say who you are and who you represent, at a natural pace. This answers the customer's first silent question: Is this safe?

Autonomy: Give the person a clear, easy way to decline or say not now. Feeling free to leave is what makes it safe to stay.

Relevance: Give one true, credible reason this contact is about them, not you. This answers: Is this relevant?

Curiosity: Ask one easy, open question that invites them to talk. This answers: Do I want to continue?, by giving them something easy to say.

Why this order matters: each step answers a question the customer is already asking. Skip one and the next one costs you. Skip Autonomy and the customer hears a trap. Skip Relevance and they hear a pitch about you.

Watch out for the most common rookie mistake: jumping from hello straight to an open question before you have given the customer a safe reason to answer it. Speed reads as pressure.

There is one more reason Autonomy works so well. When a person knows they can leave, staying feels like their choice. That choice is the first small commitment of the visit, and people naturally want to stay consistent with choices they made freely.

The Question Ladder

Once the conversation is open, most new reps make a second mistake: they present too early. They hear one detail, recognize a product, and start talking about it. The customer came with a surface statement ("just looking," "just checking prices," "I'm not sure"). Answer only the surface and you are selling blind. Now you need questions.

The Question Ladder (MEDJAY adaptation, based on Rackham) is a four-rung question structure, climbed in order: Situation, Problem, Impact, Need.

Situation: Simple facts about the customer's world. What are they looking for today? What are they replacing? Who is it for?

Problem: What isn't working now, or what do they want to avoid? What has been frustrating about the current one?

Impact: The rung most sellers skip. Ask what the problem costs them: time, money, effort, or a repeated frustration. How does this affect them day to day?

Need: Let the customer say what a good solution looks like, in their own words. Not your words. Theirs.

Why in order? Each rung builds the customer's own case in their head. By the Need rung, the customer is describing the answer while you listen. That description becomes the standard you later match your product to (you will practice that on Day 3). Climbing in order is good practice, not a rule you can never bend.

The Impact rung does the heavy lifting. A problem stated lightly feels small. A problem whose cost the customer has just described out loud feels real. People act on problems they can feel. That's System 1 at work: emotion drives the decision, and the reasoning follows.

Then there is silence. Ask your question, then wait. Do not fill the gap. A few seconds of quiet feel uncomfortable, and the reflex is to answer your own question. Don't. Silence gives the customer room to give you the honest, longer answer. Their pause is not rejection. It is thinking.

Listening: The Half of Discovery You Can't Skip

Discovery is not an interview. It is listening with your full attention so the customer feels heard. Do these four things.

Ask open questions. "What brings you in today?" opens. "Are you looking for a sofa?" closes. Use closed questions only to pin down a detail.

Let them finish. Do not jump in when you think you know the product. The last sentence usually carries the real reason.

Check your understanding back to them. "So the current one keeps losing the connection, and that's disrupting your work calls. Did I get that right?" When a person hears their own concern described back accurately, a feeling gets calmer and easier to work with. It also proves you were actually listening.

Stay neutral. If the customer mentions a competitor or a cheaper option, don't defend or compete. Ask what they liked about it. You learn the real criteria, and you don't create an argument. There is one more tool for a customer who is tense or hesitant: name it. "It sounds like you've been burned before by a bad purchase." Nobody has argued with that. A named feeling usually relaxes the person, because they no longer have to defend it. The moment a customer says "exactly," you have shifted from salesperson to someone who understands. Trust follows.

Worked Example

Practice example: A customer, Priya, walks into a home electronics store mid-afternoon. She looks at headphones, picks up two boxes, puts both down.

Weak opening: "Hi! Those are our best sellers. Excellent sound. If you buy today there's a discount." Priya says, "Just looking," and steps away.

Strong opening, using all 4 steps of the First-Contact Standard:

Low Threat: "Hi, I'm Sam, I work here. No rush at all."

Autonomy: "Take your time. If you want a hand, I'm right here. If you'd rather browse, that's fine too."

Relevance: "I noticed you comparing those two. A lot of people get stuck right at that choice."

Curiosity: "What are you planning to use them for?"

Priya answers: commuting. Sam does not pitch. He climbs the Question Ladder.

Situation: "What are you using now?"

Problem: "What isn't working about them?"

Priya: "They slip out when I run for the train, and they hurt after an hour."

Impact: "When they slip out, what happens to your call or your music?"

Priya: "I miss the start of work calls. It's happened twice this week."

Need: "So what would a good pair need to do for you?"

Priya: "Stay in when I move, be comfortable for a long commute, and sound clear for calls." Sam now has her criteria in her own words. He shows her two options that meet them, and she chooses. Notice what Sam never did: pitch first, argue, or rush.

Reflect: Which step of the strong opening did the most work? Write your answer, then compare it with how the weak opening would have felt from Priya's side.

Apply It in Your Role

In the store: position yourself so customers can approach you, and open with a calm hello. Give the easy exit ("If you need me, I'm here"), then a relevant observation, then one open question. Not working in a store yet? Watch any shop you visit. Notice how staff open a conversation. Which of the 4 steps do you hear? Which are missing? Write it down on the way home.

At the counter: when someone arrives to collect or query something, use Low Threat and Relevance quickly. "Welcome back. I see you're picking up an order. What can I sort out for you while you're here?"

On the phone: same order, slower pace. Say your name and the store, say what the call is about in one sentence, then offer the exit before your question.

No live customers yet? Role-play the phone opening with a friend or colleague, or record yourself on your phone. Play it back. Does your voice sound unhurried?

Watch for these signs of a stalled conversation: one-word answers, arms crossed, eyes on the exit. Go back down the ladder. Reassure (Low Threat), give them space (Autonomy), find a relevance point.

Note Pick one opening you hear this week and score it against the 4 steps. Watching other people open is the fastest practice when you have no customers of your own.

What Would You Say?

Write your answer to each before reading the model answers.

  1. A customer walks in and says, "I'm just browsing." What do you say next?
  2. A customer says, "I need a new washing machine, but I don't know anything about them." How do you climb the first two rungs?
  3. You've asked what a good solution looks like, and the customer goes quiet. What do you do?

Key Takeaways

  • The first seconds are about safety, not selling. The First-Contact Standard earns attention instead of demanding it.
  • Autonomy is a gift, not a risk. Freedom to leave is what makes staying feel like a choice.
  • Surface statements hide real needs. Questions find them; pitches miss them.
  • Climb the Question Ladder in order, and never skip Impact. Problems a customer has felt are problems a customer acts on.
  • Listen more than you speak, name tension, and let silence do its work.

Quiz

Answer all 5 questions. Choose one option per question. Each question is worth 20 points, 100 in total. You pass with 70 or more.

  1. What is the correct order of the First-Contact Standard?

    1. Low Threat, Autonomy, Relevance, Curiosity
    2. Relevance, Curiosity, Low Threat, Autonomy
    3. Curiosity, Relevance, Autonomy, Low Threat
    4. Low Threat, Relevance, Curiosity, Autonomy

  2. What is the main purpose of the Autonomy step?

    1. To show the customer you have a wide range of products
    2. To create urgency so the customer decides fast
    3. To give the customer a clear way to decline, which makes it safe to stay
    4. To end the conversation quickly if the customer isn't buying

  3. Which Question Ladder rung is most often skipped, and why does it matter?

    1. Situation, because facts are boring
    2. Problem, because the customer already knows it
    3. Need, because the customer should not decide
    4. Impact, because the cost of the problem is what makes it feel real

  4. A customer goes quiet after your open question. What should you do?

    1. Fill the gap with product information to keep things moving
    2. Rephrase the question immediately
    3. Wait and give them room to think
    4. Ask a closed yes/no question instead

  5. Naming a customer's concern back to them ("It sounds like you've been burned before") helps. Why?

    1. It shows you agree the store's products are faulty
    2. Naming the feeling accurately helps the customer feel understood and calmer
    3. It speeds the customer toward a purchase
    4. It gives you time to find a different product

Upskill Challenge

Do these before your next practice. Where a task needs a live store, the alternative works just as well.

  • Watch one opening in any store you visit, or play back a recording of your own practice. Write down which of the 4 steps you saw, in order, and which were missing.
  • Script your own opening for a walk-in, in your own words, using all 4 steps of the First-Contact Standard.
  • Write 3 questions for each rung of the Question Ladder, using a product you sell or a product you know well.
  • Run one opening and discovery conversation. With a real customer it is supervised by a colleague. No customers yet? Role-play it with a friend or colleague, or record yourself.
  • Afterward, write one sentence on what went well and one on which step you skipped or rushed.

Note The role-play counts. Say your opening out loud until it sounds like you, not like a page.

Model Answers

These are examples, not scripts. Your words should sound like you.

  1. "Hi, I'm (your name). Go ahead and have a look, no pressure at all. I'll be right here if you want a second opinion on anything." This gives Low Threat and Autonomy, then leaves the door open for one open question when the customer engages.
  2. "Thanks for telling me, that actually makes this easy. A couple of quick questions so I only show you what fits. What do you have now, and what's been the main frustration with it?" This is Situation, then Problem. Close with an Impact question: "When it lets you down, how does that affect your week?"
  3. Wait. Count to five in your head. If the customer still looks stuck, offer a gentle help line: "Some people care most about running costs, others about the wash cycle. Which matters more to you?" That is a genuine choice, and it makes answering easy. Practicing alone? Say the question out loud, then time your own pause with a slow count of five.

Quiz answers:

  • 1. A. (20 points) The order follows the customer's silent questions: safety, freedom to stay, relevance, then an easy invitation to talk.
  • 2. C. (20 points) Feeling free to leave is what makes it safe to stay, and staying becomes the customer's own choice.
  • 3. D. (20 points) The cost of the problem, said out loud by the customer, is what turns a small annoyance into a real reason to act.
  • 4. C. (20 points) Silence is thinking time; filling it often answers the question for the customer and loses the honest answer.
  • 5. B. (20 points) When a concern is described back accurately, the customer feels understood, and the tension around it settles.
  • Pass mark: 70 out of 100.
