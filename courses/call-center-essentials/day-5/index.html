How to Use This Guide

This is the last day of Level 1, and it is a working day, not a reading day. You will write notes, search a knowledge base, plan a reset after a hard call, and rehearse a role-play you could face in an interview or your first week on the phones.

Work through the sections in order. When you reach What Would You Say?, write your own answer first. Cover the model answers at the back of this guide until you have committed something to paper. The model answers are examples, not scripts. Your words should sound like you.

For the exercises you will need one short fictional call. If you do not have one from earlier this week, invent a short, realistic call now and write it down before you continue.

Everything here builds on what you already know. The First-Contact Standard, the Mandatory Pause and A.R.T. are yours from earlier this week. This guide shows you how to keep them alive under real working pressure.

Today's Objectives (KASU)

Knowledge
You will know what a complete call note contains, how to search a knowledge base fast, and how a 90-day plan protects a new agent from burnout.
Attitude
You will treat notes and knowledge base searches as the customer's memory, not as paperwork, and treat stress recovery as part of the job rather than a weakness.
Skill
You will be able to write a note the next agent can act on without calling you, search a knowledge base by the customer's own words and escalate a gap, and apply one behavior from quality feedback for a week.
Upskill
You will be able to structure a role-play answer out loud in an interview or assessment setting, covering opening, verification, the real issue, a calm response to an upset caller and a confirmed next step.

Notes: The Customer's Memory

The call ends when you hang up. The customer's problem does not. Your note is what carries the case forward, and a thin note is how a customer ends up repeating themselves to a stranger. Think about what the customer experiences. They told you their story once, to a person they will never speak to again. If your note is missing the detail that mattered, the next agent opens with "Can you explain the problem again?" In that moment the customer hears one thing: nobody here listens. The first-contact advantage you earned is gone.

A complete note has six parts. Write each one in this order.

  • What the customer reported, in their own words. Not your diagnosis, their description.
  • What you verified: identity, account status, order number, anything you checked and how.
  • What you did: the action taken on the account or the case.
  • What you promised, and when: the specific commitment and its date or time window.
  • What policy allowed or blocked: the rule that shaped the outcome, briefly.
  • What the next agent needs: the open question, the pending step, the name of anyone you escalated to.
  • Keep the customer's own words where they matter. "It arrived cracked and the box was wet" tells the next agent more than "damaged item." It tells them what the customer will be upset about on the next call.

Note Your test is not "does this look neat?" Your test is "could a stranger continue this case without calling me?" Write every note to pass that test.

The Knowledge Base: Fast Search, Honest Gaps

The knowledge base is why first-contact resolution is possible for ordinary people. You are not expected to hold every policy in your head. You are expected to find it quickly and use it accurately. Search in three passes. First, the customer's own words. Search the phrase they used, not the official term, because articles often carry the phrasing people actually use. Second, the product term, once you know what they are talking about. Third, the process name, such as the name of the check, the form, or the change they need.

When an article comes up, check two things before you trust it. Does the article match this customer's situation, version or product? And is the article current, with no obvious signs of being replaced? If the answer to either is no, or the article contradicts itself, do not improvise a rule. Escalate the gap.

A gap is useful information. Telling your team "this article is missing the case where the order was already shipped" improves the next hundred calls. Treating the gap as a personal failure and guessing instead is how a small case becomes a complaint.

There is a psychology to this. A customer can hear uncertainty, and they can also hear confidence built on nothing. When you say "let me confirm the exact step in our system rather than guess," you lose nothing and gain trust, because you showed them accuracy matters to you.

Searching well is not heroic. It is the quiet reason most calls end on the first contact. Agents who skip the search and answer from memory are the ones creating callbacks.

Note Common mistake: searching the official term first when the customer used a plain phrase. Start with the customer's words.

Stress: The Reset Between Calls

Call work is emotional work. You absorb many people's frustration in a single shift, and that has a cost. Researchers call the effort of managing your emotions to match your job a form of emotional labor, and it is real work, not an attitude problem. The researcher who developed the concept, Alicia Grandey (2000), frames it as something employees actively do, with real effort behind it.

Here is why "just push through" fails. Stress hormones do not clear the moment you hang up. If you carry every hard call into the next one, the tension stacks. Your voice tightens, you get faster, you answer before the customer finishes. By month three the stack is heavy, and agents who never learned to reset often leave. The job did not break them on one bad call. It wore them down on a hundred unreleased ones.

A reset takes seconds, not a break. Use the pause you already practice on hard calls, now turned outward: the seconds after a call ends. Breathe out slowly, once or twice. Name what just happened in a few words, silently or on your notepad: "angry customer, billing error, long call." Naming it that plainly lowers its charge. Then write your note while the details are fresh, and only then take the next call.

Across the shift, use your breaks and your pause at the start of each call. The first sound a customer hears should be a calm one, and a two-second breath before you speak is enough to get there. This is not time lost. It is the difference between a voice that helps and a voice that is running on empty.

Note Watch for the warning sign in yourself: fast speech, finishing the customer's sentences, a sigh before you answer. Any one of those means take one slow breath before the next call.

Quality Feedback and Coaching: One Behavior at a Time

A scorecard is a description of a call, not a verdict on you. It is easy to read one and hear "you are bad at this job." That reaction is normal, and it is also the fastest way to learn nothing from it. Read the scorecard the way you read a knowledge base article: look for what it actually says. Find the one behavior behind the lowest item. Then bring one specific question to your next coaching session. Not "how am I doing?" but "on the call where I verified before I listened, what would you have said instead in the first thirty seconds?" A specific question gets you a specific answer you can practice.

Then pick one behavior for the next week and leave the rest alone. Trying to fix five things at once means fixing none. One behavior practiced for a week shows up on the scorecard. That is how progress actually looks.

This also protects you. When you know exactly what you are working on, the number is not a judgment on you. It is information about one skill you are building.

Note In a coaching session, ask for a model sentence, not just a category. "Give me a better opening" gets you advice; "what would you have said instead of 'as I already explained'?" gets you a skill.

Interviews and Role-Plays: What Assessors Listen For

If you are job hunting, the assessment is a role-play call. The assessor is not checking whether you already know the systems. They are checking whether you have the habits. Five things catch their attention.

  • A clear opening: your name, the company, and an offer of help.
  • Verification done before any account detail is shared or changed.
  • The real issue found, not the first claim. You ask, listen, and paraphrase before solving.
  • A calm response to an upset caller. You pause, acknowledge, and do not defend.
  • A confirmed next step before the call ends, with a date or a clear action.
  • Structure your role-play answer out loud in the same order the call would run: "I would open with (A), confirm identity by (B), ask about (C) to find the real issue, if they were upset I would pause and acknowledge before responding, and I would close by confirming (D) with a date." Saying your plan out loud in call order shows the assessor you think in a process, not in slogans.
  • A: ____
  • B: ____
  • C: ____
  • D: ____
  • Practice example: In a role-play with a friend, you are the agent and your friend plays a customer whose delivery is two days late and who is already angry. Open clearly, verify identity, ask "what has happened so far?", pause before you respond to the anger, then confirm what you will do and when. Your friend then tells you one thing that made them feel helped and one thing that made them tense.

Researching a Job and Your First 90 Days

Ask about the job before you accept it. What training exists in the first two weeks? Is there a coach nearby while you take early live calls? How are quality conversations run? How does the team handle a hard call: is there a supervisor channel? The answers tell you whether the first 90 days will build you or wear you down.

Then write your own 90-day plan. Three phases.

  • Weeks 1–2: learn the systems and shadow experienced agents. Ask what they wish they had known in week one.
  • Weeks 3–6: own simple cases with a coach nearby. Ask for feedback on one behavior at a time.
  • Weeks 7–12: take harder calls, ask for a review when you feel stuck, and track two things for yourself: first contact resolution and your quality trend.
  • Track only those two for yourself at first, and use them to spot trends, not to grade yourself daily. Speed never beats a correct resolution, so do not let a low handle time become the number you chase. Balance, not one metric, is what lasts.
  • Practice example: Assume you write the two numbers in a simple note after each week, with invented figures to practice the habit: week seven shows eight of twelve cases resolved on first contact, week eight shows nine of twelve. You look for what changed in the cases that still needed a callback, not for a reason to feel bad about week seven.

Apply It in Your Role

Role-based practice, for wherever you are this week.

New on the phones: after your next live call, write the six-part note from this guide and check it against the stranger test. Would the next agent need to call you? If yes, what was missing?

Shadowing an experienced agent: listen for the moment they search the knowledge base, and note what they searched on. Compare it with the three-pass order.

Job hunting, without a phone role yet: record a mock call with a friend playing an upset customer. Then write the six-part note for that fictional call and run the stranger test from the section on notes.

Supervised practice with a coach: bring one scorecard item and one specific question to a coaching conversation, and leave with one behavior for the week.

Every exercise must be doable in your first weeks. If you have no real calls yet, a mock call with a friend is just as good practice for the habits.

What Would You Say?

Write your answer before reading the model answers at the end of this guide.

  1. A customer calls about a late delivery. You verify identity, find the order is held at a depot, and cannot confirm a delivery date. What does your note say, in the six parts?
  2. You cannot find a knowledge base article for the customer's exact case. What do you say to the customer, and what do you do next?
  3. It is the end of a hard shift. You have one hour left and the last call left you tense. What is your reset, in specific actions?
  4. An interviewer asks: "A caller is already angry and says you are useless. How would you handle that call?" Answer out loud in call order.
  5. Your coach says your scorecard is weak on verification. You want to ask one specific question. What is it?

Key Takeaways

  • Your note is the customer's memory. Write all six parts, and write to pass the stranger test.
  • Search the knowledge base in three passes: the customer's words, the product term, then the process name.
  • When an article does not fit, escalate the gap. Never guess a rule.
  • Absorbing frustration all day costs real effort. Reset in seconds, name what happened, and use your breaks.
  • Read a scorecard as a description of a call, not a verdict. Take one behavior into the next week.
  • In a role-play, talk in call order: opening, verification, real issue, calm response, confirmed next step.
  • Keep your metrics in balance. Speed never beats a correct resolution.

Quiz

Choose one answer per question, then check the answers at the end of this guide.

  1. What makes a call note complete?

    1. It contains all six parts, from the customer's words to what the next agent needs.
    2. It is short, so the next agent reads it fast.
    3. It records only your diagnosis of the problem.
    4. It records only the action you took on the account.

  2. A customer describes their problem in plain everyday words. What is the best search order?

    1. The official process name.
    2. The customer's own words first, then the product term, then the process name.
    3. Skip the knowledge base and answer from memory.
    4. The name of your supervisor.

  3. Why does "just push through" lead to burnout?

    1. It is against every company's rules.
    2. Customers can tell you are strong.
    3. Unreleased stress stacks across calls and shifts, and never clearing it wears agents down over months.
    4. Breaks are too short to matter.

  4. What is the best way to use quality feedback?

    1. Read the score as a verdict on you.
    2. Fix five scorecard items in one week.
    3. Find the one behavior behind the lowest item, ask one specific question, and practice it for a week.
    4. Ignore the scorecard and keep going.

  5. In a role-play, what do assessors most want to hear?

    1. That you know every system and policy by heart.
    2. Fast answers and quick closes.
    3. That you never pause during a call.
    4. A clear opening, verification, the real issue, a calm response, and a confirmed next step, in call order.

Upskill Challenge

Complete this before you finish the day. It has an option for everyone.

Option A, if you handle calls: take your latest real call, write the six-part note exactly as this guide describes, and run the stranger test. Ask a friend or a colleague to read only the notes and tell you whether they could continue the case without asking you anything. If they had to ask, add what was missing and write one sentence about why you left it out.

Option B, if you are job hunting or have no calls yet: create a fictional call with a friend playing an upset customer. Run a short mock call, then write the six-part note for it exactly as this guide describes. No live calls needed. Then ask your friend to run the stranger test on your notes.

Also complete this mini-plan: write your 90-day plan in three phases using the weeks 1–2, 3–6 and 7–12 structure, and choose the two numbers you will track for yourself, first contact resolution and quality trend. If you have no job yet, write the plan as the questions you will ask in interviews. Then prepare your role-play answer to What Would You Say? question 4 and say it out loud until it sounds like you, not like a script.

Write your notes for this challenge now.

What's Next: Level 2. You will build on these habits with tools for harder calls and bigger responsibilities. Until then, keep your notes complete, keep your pause, and keep practicing out loud.

Model Answers

These are examples, not scripts. Notice the principle in each one and use your own words.

  1. The note in six parts: "Customer reported: 'It was meant to be here Monday and nobody told me why.' Verified: identity and order number; order held at depot. Done: confirmed depot hold, status checked. Promised: update on delivery date by tomorrow at 5 pm. Policy: no date confirmed until depot releases; outside my authority to release. Next agent: call customer with the depot's release date, check for compensation eligibility under the policy." Why it works: it carries the customer's words, what was checked, the promise and its deadline, and the open step, so the next agent acts instead of asking the customer to start over.
  2. Say something like: "I have not found the exact case in our system, and I would rather confirm it than guess. I will check with the team who owns this and call you back by 5 pm today." Then escalate the gap with the call detail, and feed the missing case to whoever curates the knowledge base. Why it works: the customer hears accuracy, not confusion, and the gap gets fixed for the next hundred calls.
  3. The reset: set your status to wrap-up, breathe out slowly twice, write three words about the last call on your notepad, write the six-part note while it is fresh, then take one slow breath before you answer the next call. On your break, leave the desk and let your voice rest. Why it works: stress does not clear when the call ends; naming it plainly and writing the note releases it before it stacks.
  4. The role-play answer, in call order: "I would open with my name and a short offer to help, confirm identity before touching the account, then ask what has happened so far and reflect it back. If the caller is angry, I would pause before I respond, acknowledge the feeling without defending, and stay with the facts. I would search the knowledge base for the exact case, escalate a gap rather than guess, and close by confirming one next step with a date." Why it works: the assessor hears the habits that survive real calls, not slogans.
  5. The specific question: "On the calls where I shared account details before verifying identity, what would you have said in the first fifteen seconds to get verification done without slowing the call?" Then practice that one sentence all week. Why it works: a specific question produces a model sentence you can use; a general question produces general feedback.

Quiz answers:

  • 1. A. A complete note carries all six parts, so a stranger can continue the case without calling you.
  • 2. B. Start with the customer's phrase, then the product term, then the process name, and escalate a gap rather than guess.
  • 3. C. The effort of managing your emotions is real work, and unreleased stress stacks over hundreds of calls and wears agents down by month three.
  • 4. C. One behavior at a time is how progress shows up; a verdict or five fixes at once teaches nothing.
  • 5. D. Assessors listen for habits in call order: opening, verification, real issue, calm response, and a confirmed next step.
