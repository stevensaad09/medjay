How to Use This Guide

Anger on a call is information, not an attack on you. Today you learn what to do with that information.

Read each section once, then do the exercise before you look at the model answers at the end. Write your answers down, even roughly. You will keep more of this if your hand moves than if your eyes only pass over the page.

You do not need a job to practice any of this. Every exercise works with a friend playing an angry customer, a recording of your own mock call, or your memory of being the customer in a bad service moment. Use those.

Keep one thing in mind all day: the customer's anger is about their loss, not your worth. Today is about staying steady while you find out what the loss is, and then moving the case forward.

Note Read this page, then stop and do the first exercise in Today's Objectives before you read on.

Why Anger Arrives Before You Do

A customer who calls you angry has usually already lost something. Time, money, a plan for the day, or the belief that the company treats them fairly. On Day 1 you learned that the customer often starts the contact already carrying a loss. Today you deal with the version of that customer who is not hiding it.

Here is the part most new representatives miss. The anger is aimed at the situation, but it lands on you because you are the person on the line. Your brain hears it as a threat to you. Your body speeds up, your voice rises, and you start defending. None of that helps the customer, and it makes the case harder to settle.

Non-defensive neutrality is the skill of staying calm and factual while the anger passes through you. You do not agree with a claim you have not checked. You do not argue. You stay steady and keep gathering what you need. Think of yourself as a wall the wave hits: the wave is real, the wall does not move.

Practice example: a customer calls a parcel service and says her delivery has been "lost for the third time this month" and that nobody there "does their job." The representative has not yet checked the tracking. A defensive reply starts with "We do our best" or "That's not our fault." A neutral reply keeps the facts open: "It sounds like this has happened more than once and you have had to chase it each time."

Why does the wave land on you? Because a fast, angry voice triggers the fast, automatic kind of thinking that Day 1 described. Your instinct is to protect yourself first. The customer's instinct, on their side of the line, is to be heard before they can think about a solution. If you move to facts while they are still unheard, they push harder. That is not stubbornness; it is a person trying to get a basic need met.

Note Watch for the moment you want to say "actually." That word usually starts a defense.

Naming the Feeling Out Loud

There is a simple, well-studied move that lowers the temperature: say the customer's feeling out loud, in plain words. Researchers call this affect labeling. Lieberman and colleagues found that putting feelings into words is linked to a calmer response to emotional pictures. You do not need the brain science to use it. You need the habit.

Labeling sounds like this: "It sounds like you are frustrated." "It seems like you feel like nobody has taken this seriously." "It appears this has cost you a lot of time." Notice what these have in common. They start with a soft phrase, not with "I" or "We," and they describe the customer's experience, not your side of it.

Why does saying a feeling out loud calm it? A person in a strong emotion is often trying to get someone to acknowledge it. When you name it accurately, they no longer have to push so hard to be heard. Some of the pressure comes off. They often answer with something like "Yes, exactly," and then the real information starts to come.

A label is not an apology and not an agreement with every claim. You can label a feeling without accepting a version of events you have not checked. "It sounds like you feel let down" is not the same as "We let you down." The first is safe to say at any point.

A good label is short. Do not chain three feelings into one sentence. Do not add "but" after it; the word "but" erases everything in front of it. If you catch yourself saying "I understand, but," stop and try the label alone.

Note If your first label is wrong, the customer will correct you, and that correction is useful. Say "thank you for telling me" and try again.

A.R.T.: Moving a Complaint Forward

When a complaint is in front of you, use A.R.T., MEDJAY's own framework for handling complaints and concerns. It has 3 steps, and you use them in order. Always run all three together; skipping one is where complaints get stuck.

A is Acknowledge. Label the customer's feeling, with no "but" after it. "It sounds like you are angry about the wait, and you have every right to feel that way." This is the step from the section above, and it comes first for a reason. A customer who has not been acknowledged is not ready to hear anything else.

R is Reframe. Shift the conversation from the visible cost, what already went wrong, to what settling it now gives them, and honestly to what staying stuck costs them. Do not defend features or the company. Do not blame another team. For example: "The delay has already cost you this morning. What I can do now is get you a confirmed new time so the rest of your day is not spent waiting."

That is honest: it names a real cost, then points at a real gain.

T is Transition. Ask a calibrated how or what question. Chris Voss, in his work on negotiation, calls these calibrated questions. Never a yes/no question, and never a trade or concession. "What would you need to see to feel confident about tomorrow's delivery?" is a Transition. "Shall I book that for you?" is not.

Here is a full A.R.T. chain, all 3 steps labeled, on a mock call.

Practice example: a customer calls a broadband provider and says the internet has been out since yesterday and that he works from home. He says the representative is "the third person who has not helped."

Acknowledge: "It sounds like this has been going on for two days and you feel like nobody has actually helped you yet. That is a long time to be without connection while you are trying to work."

Reframe: "The last two days are gone. What I can do right now is find out exactly what is blocking the line and give you a real time by which it will be back, so you can plan your work calls around it."

Transition: "What would you need from me today to feel this is finally being handled?"

Why does this work? The customer arrives with two problems: the outage, and the feeling of being passed around. Acknowledging handles the feeling. Reframing moves attention to a future the customer can act on. The Transition hands control back to him, which is the same idea you practiced on Day 2 with the First-Contact Standard's Autonomy step: people stay engaged when they have some control over how the contact runs.

One warning you will meet on the job: it is easy to skip straight to Reframe, or even to a suggestion, without acknowledging first. That lands as a brush-off, not a resolution. If a customer ever says "you are not listening," check whether you skipped step one.

Note In your own words, A.R.T. reads as: hear it, turn it toward what settling it now gives them, then ask how or what.

What Escalates a Complaint

Three habits make an angry customer louder. Learn to see them in yourself early.

Arguing the facts first. The customer says the delivery was late; you say the tracking shows it was on time. You may be right. But leading with facts before the feeling is heard tells the customer you are defending yourself. Do the Acknowledge step first, then check the facts together.

A scripted apology. "I apologize for any inconvenience" said in a flat tone makes a customer angrier, because it sounds copied. The customer hears a wall of words, not a person. A specific acknowledgment does more than a general apology: "It sounds like the wait this morning has made you late for something." It shows you listened to this case, not a template.

Over-promising. "It will definitely be fixed today" is a promise you cannot check. You learned this on Day 1 and it matters most under pressure: never promise anything that has not been verified. If a customer has been let down before, one broken promise adds to a stack of them. Say what you know, and say clearly what you do not know yet: "I do not have a confirmed time yet. What I can do is check and call you back once I do. What number is best for that?" Honest, and still moving. Why does anger grow in these moments? Pressure creates resistance. When a customer feels pushed toward a conclusion, a fact, or a promise, they push back to keep some control.

Note Under pressure, slowdown beats speedup. Speak a little slower than you feel.

Handing a Case to a Supervisor

Some cases need someone with more authority, and handing them over well is part of the job. The handover goes wrong when the customer feels passed around again, or when they hear the representative speak about them as a problem.

Hand over cleanly with four moves. First, tell the customer why and what happens next, in plain words: "This needs a decision above my level, and the next step is that I bring in someone who can make it. That usually takes one short conversation." Second, stay on the line and introduce the case in neutral words, without blame: "This is a customer whose service has been out for two days and who has been in touch before; here is what has been checked so far." Third, give the customer control over how it happens: "What would help most right now, to speak to my supervisor directly, or to get a confirmed callback time?" Fourth, confirm what was agreed before you leave the call. That third move matters. Handing over is not telling the customer to leave. It is a real choice over how they get their problem fixed. Keep it that way.

Note Never say a supervisor will "deal with" the customer. Say what the supervisor can actually decide.

Apply It in Your Role

You may not have a shift yet. You can still do every part of this today.

On a call center seat, you would use A.R.T. on the first angry contact of the day and note which step felt hardest. In chat or email, you would write the Acknowledge line first and check it has no "but." At a service counter, you would keep your voice steady and your sentences short while a customer raises theirs. In a school project, a volunteer role, or any group where you are the one at the desk, the same moves apply.

Choose one setting and picture the moment today's skill would matter. Write two lines: the setting, and the first sentence you would say.

Note Keep the picture specific. A vague setting gives you nothing to practice.

What Would You Say?

Write your answer to each situation before you look at the model answers. Use your own words. The model answers are examples, not scripts.

  1. A customer opens a call with "This is the fourth time I have called and you are all useless." What is your first label?
  2. A customer at a counter says, "I paid for next-day delivery and it arrived four days late." Write all 3 steps of A.R.T. You do not have authority to refund anything.
  3. A customer asks you for a time when the repair will be finished. You have no confirmed time yet. What two sentences do you say?
  4. A customer is now shouting and will not let you finish a sentence. You believe the case needs a supervisor. What do you say, and what choice do you offer?

Note Time yourself. Two minutes per answer, on paper, then compare.

Key Takeaways

  • The customer's anger is about their loss, not your worth. Stay steady and factual; the wave passes through.
  • Naming the feeling out loud, plainly and without "but," lowers the heat. It is not an apology and not an agreement with every claim.
  • A.R.T., MEDJAY's own framework for handling complaints and concerns, has 3 steps used in order: Acknowledge, Reframe, Transition. Run all three together.
  • The Reframe moves attention to what settling it now gives them and, honestly, what staying stuck costs. No defending, no blame.
  • The Transition is always a calibrated how or what question, never yes/no, never a trade.
  • Three things escalate: arguing facts first, a scripted apology, over-promising. Slow down under pressure.
  • Never promise anything that has not been verified.
  • Hand over to a supervisor with four moves: tell them why, stay and introduce, offer a real choice of how, confirm what was agreed.

Quiz

    1. "I apologize for any inconvenience."
    2. "It sounds like you feel like you have been left to sort this out alone."
    3. "Actually, the notes show we called on Monday."
    4. "I understand, but let me explain what happened."

  2. A.R.T. has 3 steps. In what order do you use them?

    1. Reframe, Acknowledge, Transition.
    2. Transition, Reframe, Acknowledge.
    3. Acknowledge, Reframe, Transition.
    4. Acknowledge, Transition, argue the facts.

  3. Which line is a Transition as taught on this day?

    1. "Shall I put you on hold while I check?"
    2. "Can I send you the confirmation now?"
    3. "Would you like me to book the new slot?"
    4. "What would you need to see to feel this is settled today?"

  4. A customer demands a confirmed repair time and you have none yet. What is the best reply?

    1. "It will definitely be done today." B. "I will find out and call you back. What number is best for that?"
    2. "That's not my department."
    3. "I apologize for any inconvenience."

  5. A case needs a decision above your level. Which handover is clean?

    1. "You will have to speak to my supervisor. Please hold."
    2. "My supervisor will deal with you now."
    3. "This needs a decision above my level. Would you rather speak to my supervisor now, or get a confirmed callback time?" D. "There is nothing more I can do."

Upskill Challenge

Complete the 10-Round Interruption Drill with a friend or a recording. It is a structured inbound service drill of ten rounds using scripted customer replies in order: rounds 1–3 the customer interrupts angrily and you Acknowledge; rounds 4–6 you run the full A.R.T. chain; rounds 7–8 the customer demands a supervisor and you hand over with the four moves; rounds 9–10 the customer wants to end the contact before it is resolved, and you respect that, confirm what is recorded on the case and when they will hear next. Score each round PASS or FAIL.

Why the last four rounds matter: telling when to stop is a service skill, not a failure. A customer who wants to end the contact before it is resolved has made a decision, and respecting it is the steady, factual response. Confirm what is recorded on the case and when they will hear next, then let them go.

Run the drill once and score it. Then run it again the next day and compare. Bring the difference, not the score, to your own review.

Before that, do the partner version. Ask a friend to play an angry customer for five minutes on a complaint you invent together. Use all 3 steps of A.R.T. out loud and write down what your partner says the moment they first felt heard. If no partner is available, record a mock call on your phone and listen back for your Acknowledge line.

Note In the drill, watch the moment you want to defend yourself. A steady voice and a plain label do more than a fast answer.

Model Answers

These are examples, not scripts. Your words should sound like you; the principle matters more than the exact wording.

  1. "It sounds like this has happened more than once and you feel like nobody there is doing their job." It labels the feeling and the repeat, uses a soft opener, and adds no "but." Why it works: the customer hears that the runaround itself has been noticed, without you accepting a claim you have not checked.
  2. Acknowledge: "It seems like you are frustrated; you paid for next-day and it came four days late, and that is a real annoyance." Reframe: "The four days are gone. What I can do is get you a confirmed answer on the delivery cost today so you are not left waiting again." Transition: "What would make this feel sorted for you today?" Why it works: all 3 steps run in order, with no promise about money and no defense of the carrier.
  3. "I do not have a confirmed time yet, and I will not guess." "What I can do is check with the repair team and call you back once I have a real time. What number is best for that?" Why it works: it keeps the rule on verified promises and still moves the case forward.
  4. "This needs a decision above my level, and I want to get you to someone who can make it." Then offer the choice: "What would help most right now, to speak to my supervisor now, or to get a confirmed callback time?" Why it works: the reason is plain, the choice is real, and the customer keeps control over how their problem gets fixed.

Quiz answers:

  • 1. B. It names the customer's feeling with a soft opener and no "but"; A and D are the scripted and defensive replies, and C argues facts.
  • 2. C. A.R.T. is Acknowledge, then Reframe, then Transition, always in that order and together.
  • 3. D. Only D is a calibrated how or what question; A, B and C are yes/no questions.
  • 4. B. It says plainly what is not known yet and asks for what is needed to reach the customer; A breaks the rule on verified promises.
  • 5. C. It gives the reason, offers a genuine choice over how it happens, and keeps the customer in control.

Note Compare your answers to these and note one phrase you will borrow and one you will change to sound like you.
