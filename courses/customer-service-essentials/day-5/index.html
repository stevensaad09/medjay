How to Use This Guide

This is the last day of Level 1, and it is the day everything comes together. You have learned how to open a contact, find the real problem, stay steady with an angry customer, and write messages that hold up. Today you learn how to end a contact well.

Work through the sections in order. Write your answers before you read the model answers at the back. Say the sentences out loud, because a close you have never spoken is a close you cannot deliver.

You need no job and no customers to practice. A friend, an AI partner, or your own voice recorder is enough. Every exercise has an option that fits where you are right now.

Keep one rule in your head all day. A contact ends with a how or what question, or a real choice with a date. Never with a yes or no question.

Today's Objectives (KASU)

Knowledge
You will know the closing sequence for a simple ending, the 5 steps of the Commitment Close, and when to use each one.
Attitude
You will treat an ending as part of the service, not as an escape from the conversation.
Skill
You will be able to end a contact with a how or what question, and keep a promise with a short update even when there is no news.
Upskill
You will run the full arc of a contact with a friend or an AI partner — open, discover, steady, resolve, close — and record it, then score your own ending.

Two Ways to End a Contact

Most contacts end simply. The problem is solved, the customer understands what happens next, and the conversation ends. That ending still needs structure, or it fizzles.

The simple ending has three moves. First, confirm what was resolved, in one sentence. Second, say what happens next and when. Third, ask a how or what question that hands the ending to the customer.

Here is how that sounds.

Practice example: "So the replacement part is ordered and it arrives Thursday. You will get a message when it ships. What else would you like me to check before we finish?"

Notice what that last question does. It is not "Is there anything else?" — a yes or no question the customer can answer with a quick "no" while still unsure. "What else would you like me to check" invites the customer to think, and it gives them the last word on whether the contact is complete. The psychology matters here. A customer who feels rushed out of a conversation remembers the rush, not the fix. The last thirty seconds are what they carry away. A calm, structured ending tells them the whole contact was handled, not just the middle of it.

There is a second reason. Customers often hold back a small extra concern until the end, because they do not want to seem difficult. A how or what question at the end gives that concern a door to walk through. A yes or no question keeps the door shut.

Write your own simple ending now. Use a fictional problem, any problem you like.

Your confirmed resolution: ____ Your next step and timing: ____ Your how or what question: ____

When the Customer Must Choose: The Commitment Close

Some contacts cannot end with a simple confirmation, because the customer has to pick between real options. A replacement or a refund. One delivery date or another. A repair now or a new unit later.

This is where the Commitment Close (MEDJAY adaptation, based on Voss, Kahneman, Dixon and Adamson, and Cialdini) belongs. It is MEDJAY's close: the logical conclusion of the process, not pressure, and the customer can still say no. It has exactly 5 steps: Criteria, Evidence, Consequence, Calibrated question, Genuine choice.

Criteria: Restate the customer's own criteria, in their words, from earlier in the contact.

Evidence: Show the evidence against those criteria — what you know, what is confirmed, what is not.

Consequence: Name the consequences honestly, including when waiting is better. If waiting a week would genuinely serve them, say so.

Calibrated question: Ask a What or How question. Never a yes or no question.

Genuine choice: Offer real choices of the next step, with a date.

Here is the whole chain in one contact.

Practice example: A customer's washing machine has failed twice in a month. She said she needs something reliable before her sister's family visits in three weeks, and she does not want to keep taking time off for repairs.

Criteria: "You told me reliability before the visit matters more than anything, and you cannot keep losing workdays."

Evidence: "This model has failed twice in four weeks. The replacement unit you are considering has a different pump design, and I can see two units in stock at the depot."

Consequence: "If we repair again, the earliest slot is in nine days, which leaves you two weeks before the visit. If we swap it, the new unit can be delivered this Friday. Waiting is only better if you would rather keep this model, and honestly I would not."

Calibrated question: "What would you need to see to feel confident in the swap?"

Genuine choice: "Would you rather have the swap delivered this Friday, or the repair on the earliest slot in nine days?"

Read that again and notice three things. There is no defending of features. There is an honest line about when waiting is better. And the ending is a real choice with a date, not "Shall we go ahead?" Why does this work on the customer's side? Because a customer facing a choice between resolution options is not deciding whether they like you. They are trying to avoid picking wrong. When you restate their own criteria, you hand them back their own reasoning, and the decision starts to feel like theirs rather than yours. The genuine choice at the end protects that feeling. A customer who picks the Friday swap owns the Friday swap.

The Commitment Close is used only when the customer chooses between resolution options. For everything else — a simple ending, a transfer, a follow-up call — use the simple ending above. Using the heavy version on a light contact makes the customer feel processed.

Keeping Promises When Nothing Has Changed

You made a promise. Two days later, nothing has happened yet. Do you contact the customer? Yes. A short update with no news still counts as a deposit into the relationship. Silence, on the other hand, gets spent like a withdrawal, because the customer's imagination fills the gap with the worst possibility.

Here is the shape of a no-news update. Say who you are and what the promise was. Say what has and has not happened. Say when you will next be in touch.

Practice example: "Hi, this is a message for Mr. Okafor about the refund we discussed on Monday. There is no change yet — the refund is still being processed and I do not have a date from the finance team beyond the five working days I quoted. I will message you again on Thursday whether or not there is news."

Notice the honesty in "I do not have a date." You did not invent one. Never promise anything that has not been verified, and that applies most sharply when you are tempted to fill silence with reassurance.

The psychology is simple and it is the reason this habit matters. People weigh a broken promise far more heavily than they weigh a kept one. A researcher named Baumeister and his colleagues reviewed a large body of evidence and found that bad events tend to have a stronger effect than good events of similar size. One silence at the wrong moment can outweigh several good contacts before it.

So a no-news update is not administration. It is the moment the customer decides whether you are someone whose word means something.

Write a no-news update for a fictional case. Keep it under fifty words.

Your update: ____

Inviting Honest Feedback Without Fishing for Praise

At the end of a contact, you can ask for feedback. The trap is asking in a way that only allows praise. "Was everything okay today?" invites a polite yes.

Ask instead something the customer can answer honestly. "What could we have done better today?" gives them permission to say something real. So does "How would you describe this contact to a colleague?"

Some customers will still say everything was fine. That is their right, and it is not your job to extract criticism. Your job is to leave the door open and then listen without defending.

If the feedback is negative, do not explain, justify or correct. Say thank you, say what you heard, and say what you will do with it. "Thank you — you are telling me the wait was the worst part, not the repair. I will pass that on." That sentence costs nothing and tells the customer their honesty was worth the risk.

Why does this matter for you, before you have a job? Because interviewers and experienced supervisors can hear the difference between someone who fishes for praise and someone who can sit with a real answer. It is one of the clearest signals of service temperament.

What Good Recovery Can and Cannot Do

Recovery means what you do after something has gone wrong. A missed callback. A wrong part. A long wait. Recovery is a skill, and it can be learned.

Here is what the research tells you, honestly. Studies of service recovery, including work by de Matos and colleagues, have found that a well-handled failure can leave customers more satisfied than they were before. That is real, and it is why recovery matters.

Here is the part nobody tells you, and you should hold onto it. Good recovery is remembered and can rebuild trust, but it never guarantees more loyalty than if nothing had gone wrong. Some advice you will hear implies that a brilliant apology makes a customer yours forever. That claim goes beyond what the evidence supports. A recovered customer may end up as satisfied as a customer who never had a problem — not reliably more.

So do not treat recovery as a trick for earning extra credit. Treat it as returning a customer to where they were before the failure, which is already a great outcome. And do not blame yourself when a customer stays cool after a perfect recovery. You cannot undo the fact that the failure happened. You can only make the ending honest.

This also explains why the promises you keep matter so much in the first place. The cheapest recovery is the one you never need.

In one or two sentences, describe a time you were the customer and something went wrong. Write what the person did and how you felt afterward.

What happened and what they did: ____ How you felt afterward: ____

Apply It in Your Role

You do not have a shift yet, so apply this where you are.

As a caller: the next time you contact any service line, listen to how it ends. Note the exact words of the last question the person asks you. Judge whether it was a how or what question or a yes or no question.

As a role-player: run a five-minute mock call with a friend. You are the representative. The friend plays a customer with a fictional faulty appliance and a deadline. Practice the simple ending first, then the Commitment Close with a real choice of two delivery dates.

As a writer: draft three endings on paper — one simple ending, one full Commitment Close in 5 steps, one no-news update. Read them aloud. Cut any sentence that defends a feature or asks for a yes.

As an observer: watch a recorded service call, a demonstration video, or a customer service scene in any film. Score the ending out of ten and write one line on what you would change.

Watch out for one common mistake. It is tempting to jump straight to "here is what we can offer" when a customer raises a concern about timing or options. That lands as a brush-off, not a resolution. Use the steps in order.

Using Today's Skills in a Job Interview

You will be asked about difficult customers in almost any customer service interview. You do not need a job history to answer well. You need one real story and a clear structure.

Use a moment when you were the customer: a delivery that went wrong, a refund that stalled, a call where nobody listened. Or use a practice role-play you have run with a friend. Say which one it is, plainly, and then tell it in four beats. What the person in front of you needed. What you did first. What you did when it got harder. How it ended. Keep it to about a minute, and end on the ending, not on how hard it was.

The interviewer is not testing whether you have handled a thousand cases. They are listening for whether you stay steady, whether you listen before you act, and whether you can end a contact cleanly.

If the interview includes a role-play, use what you already know. When the interviewer plays an annoyed customer and raises a cost or a wait, use A.R.T., MEDJAY's own framework — acknowledge, reframe, transition — as one chain, exactly as you practiced it on Day 3. Do not defend a feature. When the scene reaches its end, use the simple ending from today: confirm what was resolved, say what happens next and when, and ask a how or what question.

Practice example: the interviewer, in role, says the repair is too expensive. You say, "It sounds like the cost is landing at a bad moment. What matters more here is getting the machine working before the weekend. What would help you decide between the repair and the swap?" That is acknowledge, reframe, transition, followed by a question the customer can genuinely answer.

Then ask your own question. Try: "How does the team keep customers updated when a case is waiting on another department?" It shows you already think about promises and case notes, which is the part of the job most candidates never mention.

Note One honest story beats three polished ones. If you use a practice role-play, say so — interviewers respect a candidate who practices.

What Would You Say?

Write your answer to each situation before you look at the model answers. Keep them general. The words should sound like you, not like a script.

  1. A customer's repair is booked for Tuesday and she has confirmed it twice. What is your simple ending?
  2. A customer must choose between a refund that takes five working days and a store credit available today. He needs the money for a purchase next week. Write the full Commitment Close in 5 steps.
  3. You promised to call a customer about a delivery on Thursday, and it is Thursday morning with no news. Write your update.
  4. A customer says the wait was "terrible" at the end of a resolved contact. How do you respond?
  5. A customer asks, after a well-handled failure, "So do I get something extra for the trouble?" How do you answer honestly without promising anything unverified?

Key Takeaways

  • A contact ends with a how or what question, or a real choice with a date, never a yes or no question.
  • The simple ending has three moves: confirm what was resolved, say what happens next and when, ask a how or what question.
  • The Commitment Close has exactly 5 steps — Criteria, Evidence, Consequence, Calibrated question, Genuine choice — and is used only when the customer chooses between resolution options.
  • A short update with no news still counts as a deposit, because bad events weigh more heavily than good ones of similar size.
  • Invite honest feedback with questions that allow a real answer, and never defend when you get one.
  • Good recovery is remembered and can rebuild trust, but it never guarantees more loyalty than if nothing had gone wrong.

Upskill Challenge

Run the full arc of a contact with a friend or an AI partner, and record it.

Set up a fictional case with a real choice in it. For example, a faulty appliance and a deadline, with a repair or a replacement to choose between. Write the case down in three lines before you start. Run the contact from beginning to end: opening, finding the real problem, staying steady if the customer gets frustrated, and then the ending. Use the Commitment Close for the choice, in all 5 steps, ending on a real choice with a date.

Record it on your phone or through your AI partner's transcript. Listen back once, and score yourself on four lines.

  • Did I restate the customer's own criteria in their words?
  • Did my last question ask how or what, or offer a real choice with a date?
  • Did I defend any feature or ask for a yes?
  • Did I keep at least one promise inside the recording — a time I said I would do something, and then did it?

Date of recording: ____ One line to change next time: ____ Then repeat the recording once with that change. The second recording is the one that counts.

Model Answers

These are examples, not scripts. What matters is the principle behind each one, and your words should sound like you.

  1. Simple ending. "So the repair is booked for Tuesday between nine and eleven, and you will get a reminder message on Monday. What would make the visit easier for you?" Why it works: it confirms, states timing, and ends on a how or what question that gives the customer the last word.
  2. Full Commitment Close. Practice example: "You told me the money is needed for a purchase next week and that you cannot wait long. The refund takes five working days, which clears the middle of next week; the store credit is available today. If the purchase falls before the refund clears, waiting costs you the timing you told me about. What would you need to know about the credit to feel comfortable with it? Would you rather take the store credit today, or the refund in five working days?" Why it works: all 5 steps appear in order, the consequence is honest, and the ending is a real choice between the two resolution options.
  3. No-news update. "Hi, this is a message about the delivery we discussed. There is no change yet, and I do not have a confirmed date. I will call you Friday morning whether or not there is news." Why it works: it states honestly what is not known and commits to the next contact.
  4. Responding to negative feedback. "Thank you for saying that — the wait was the worst part, not the repair itself. I will pass that on, because it is useful." Why it works: it names what was heard, does not defend, and treats the feedback as worth something.
  5. Answering the "what do I get extra" question. "I understand why you are asking. I cannot offer anything beyond what is confirmed in your case today, and I would rather tell you that honestly than promise something I cannot check. What is still unresolved for you?" Why it works: it refuses politely, explains the refusal, and returns to the customer's real concern with a calibrated question.

Quiz answers:

  • 1. A. It confirms the resolution, states the next step with timing, and ends on a how or what question, not a yes or no question.
  • 2. C. The 5 steps are Criteria, Evidence, Consequence, Calibrated question, Genuine choice, in that order.
  • 3. B. A short update with no news still counts as a deposit, and you never promise anything unverified.
  • 4. B. Thanking, reflecting and stating what you will do invites honesty without defending.
  • 5. D. Recovery can rebuild trust and is worth doing, but the claim that it reliably beats no failure goes beyond the evidence.
