How to Use This Guide

You have spent six days finding the right person, opening the conversation, and asking questions that reveal real problems. Today you learn how to present your offer so it lands. Work through this guide alone, with a pen and a notebook open. Write your answers down before you check the model answers at the end. Say your offer out loud, because A.I.M. only works when it sounds like you talking, not like a page being read.

  • Set aside about 40 minutes. Read one section, then do the short exercise under it.
  • Keep the facts you gathered in discovery in front of you: the customer's exact words about their problem.
  • Practice every example out loud. Your voice teaches you where the offer gets vague.
  • Finish with the Quiz and the Upskill Challenge. The challenge is real work with real people, not reading.

Note The biggest mistake at this stage is rushing to the presentation. If you have not done discovery, you have nothing to anchor on. Go back to Day 4 first.

Today's Objectives (KASU)

Here is what you will take away from today. Read the four objectives, then come back to them at the end and score yourself honestly.

Knowledge
You will know A.I.M., MEDJAY's framework for structuring an offer, and why one clear reason is remembered while ten features are forgotten.
Attitude
You will shift from wanting to prove how much your offer does to wanting the customer to understand one thing worth acting on.
Skill
You will be able to deliver a short A.I.M. offer that anchors the problem in the customer's own words, illuminates one benefit, and asks for one small next step.
Upskill
You will use A.I.M. with three real customers and identify which of the three steps felt weakest in practice.

Note If you can only remember one thing today, remember this: the customer's words, not yours, are the raw material of a good offer.

The Rookie Mistake: Ten Features, One Memory

The rookie instinct is to list everything your product does. You are proud of it, you built it, and it feels safer to say more. The customer hears none of it. When you give ten reasons, the customer remembers one or none, and you have handed them an easy exit: they need to think about it. Think about why they do this. A person deciding whether to hand over money is already carrying doubt. A wall of features adds work to that decision, so their mind does the cheapest thing available and postpones it. One clear reason does the opposite. It gives them something small and specific to hold onto, and it makes saying yes feel lighter than saying nothing. That is the buyer psychology behind A.I.M.: people do not act on the full case. They act on the one point that connects to the thing bothering them most.

  • More information does not create more confidence. It creates more to weigh, and weighing feels like effort.
  • The customer is not comparing your features. They are asking one silent question: will this fix my problem?
  • One reason, said simply and well, is easier to remember, easier to repeat to someone else, and easier to say yes to.

Note Watch for over-explaining in yourself. If you catch yourself stacking reasons, stop and return to the single problem the customer named.

A.I.M.: MEDJAY's Framework for Structuring an Offer

A.I.M. is MEDJAY's framework for structuring an offer, developed by Steven Saad. It has three steps, used in order: A: Anchor: name the customer's specific pain or gap in their own words. I: Illuminate: show the one thing that removes that pain, with proof the customer can check. M: Mobilize: ask for one small, easy, useful next step. Use A.I.M. only for presenting an offer. Each step does one job, and the order matters. Anchor first, because a person only listens to a solution once they feel understood. Illuminate second, with a single benefit and something the customer can check for themselves, because claims are cheap but evidence closes the gap. Mobilize last, with the smallest step that moves things forward, because a large request triggers caution while a small one triggers curiosity.

  • A: Anchor: name the customer's specific pain or gap in their own words.
  • I: Illuminate: show the one thing that removes that pain, with proof the customer can check.
  • M: Mobilize: ask for one small, easy, useful next step.

Note Never skip the Anchor. If you jump straight to the benefit, the customer has to work out whether it even applies to them, and most will not bother.

Worked Example: A.I.M. in a B2B Offer

You own a small business that builds scheduling tools for other small companies. Practice example: your customer runs a five-location cleaning company and told you, in discovery, that she loses two or three jobs a week because bookings sit in a spreadsheet and a dispatcher misses them. Your A.I.M. offer sounds like this. Anchor: When we spoke, you said the real frustration is that bookings sit in a spreadsheet and two or three jobs slip away every week. Illuminate: My setup moves every booking into one shared calendar that each of your five offices sees instantly, so the same slot cannot be sold twice, and I will show you that live on your own data before you pay anything.

Mobilize: Would it work to start with a paid pilot on one of your locations this week, with a set scope, success criteria and duration we agree up front, so you can watch it work before you commit to the rest?

Note Notice the seller does not mention five dashboards, mobile apps, or reporting depth. Those may matter later. Today they would only crowd out the one reason she acts on. A paid pilot is also a real commitment: it tells you whether she means it, and it pays you for the learning. - Anchor uses her words: spreadsheet, jobs slipping, every week. - Illuminate offers one benefit, no double bookings, plus proof she can check: a live demonstration on her own data. - Mobilize asks for a paid pilot on one location, with scope, success criteria and duration agreed first. Small, clear and paid.

Worked Example: A.I.M. in a B2C Offer

You run a small service selling and installing home water filters. Practice example: your customer told you the water in the area tastes strongly of chemicals and her children refuse to drink it, so she buys bottled water every week. Anchor: You said the water tastes of chemicals and the kids won't drink it, so you are carrying bottled water home every week. Illuminate: This filter fits under the sink and you drink normal tap water the same day, and I can test your water in front of you before anything is installed. Mobilize: Would you like me to test your water now, so you can see the numbers before you decide?

  • Anchor repeats her own details: taste, children, bottled water.
  • Illuminate is one outcome, drinkable tap water today, with proof she can watch.
  • Mobilize is a free test, the smallest possible step toward a decision.

Note In B2C, customers often cannot judge specifications, so the proof they can check matters even more. Let them see or touch the evidence rather than hearing about it.

Why It Works: Contrast and Small Commitments

Two pieces of buyer psychology sit under A.I.M. First, contrast shapes judgment. A price or a decision feels different depending on what it is compared with. When you Illuminate, you are choosing the comparison for the customer: not your price against a competitor's price, but the cost of their problem against the cost of the fix. Use their real numbers, never invented ones. Second, small commitments pull people forward. A customer who agrees to a small, useful step, like a water test or a one-location trial, is now involved. They have acted, and people prefer to stay consistent with what they have already done. This is why Mobilize asks for something small. The goal is not to trap anyone. The step must be genuinely easy and genuinely useful, so that a yes today is honest and a no is respected.

  • Contrast: choose the comparison honestly. Their problem's cost against the fix's cost, using their own figures.
  • Small commitments: a real, useful first step makes the next one easier, because people follow through on what they have already started.

Note If you have to invent numbers to make the contrast work, the offer is not ready. Go back and get the real ones.

Reflection

Take five minutes with your notebook. These questions are for you alone, so be honest. The answers tell you where your offer presentation will break under pressure.

  • Write the last offer you presented in a real conversation. Which step of A.I.M. did you actually do, and which did you skip?
  • What proof can a customer check for themselves about your offer, without taking your word for it?
  • What is the smallest useful next step you could ask for this week, and why have you been asking for something bigger?

Note If you cannot name proof a customer can check, that is your priority for the next 24 hours.

Apply It in Your Role

You run your own business, so you present offers yourself, often on the phone, in a message, or face to face. Build one A.I.M. template you can say in under 45 seconds, then adapt it for each conversation. Keep the customer's own words for the Anchor, choose the single most important benefit for Illuminate, and name proof they can verify. For the Mobilize step, keep a ready list of small next steps: a short call, a trial of one team or location, a demonstration, a sample, a test, a walk-through. Pick one that is easy to say yes to and useful even if they never buy.

  • Write your Anchor sentence using the exact words three real customers used about their problem.
  • Choose the one benefit you will illuminate for your most common problem, and drop the rest for now.
  • Write down the proof a customer can check: a demonstration, a test, a sample, a reference, a try-before-buy.
  • List three small Mobilize steps you can offer, each doable within a week.
  • Record yourself saying your A.I.M. offer. Listen once. Cut anything that is not the three steps.

Note Keep your offer short enough to say in one breath per step. If you run out of breath, the customer runs out of patience.

What Would You Say?

Write your answer to each situation before you look at the model answers at the end of this guide. There are no perfect scripts here, only patterns. Your wording should sound like you.

  • A B2B prospect told you her team wastes two days every month building a report manually. Write your Anchor and Illuminate in two sentences.
  • A B2C customer said he is embarrassed to invite guests over because his garden is out of control. Write your Mobilize step as one question.
  • A customer asks, right after your offer, 'So what exactly do I get for that price?' Write one sentence that anchors on the problem again rather than listing features.
  • Write your own full A.I.M. offer for your business, three sentences, one per step.

Note Answer in your own words first, then compare. The principle matters, not the exact phrasing.

Key Takeaways

  • This is the summary to keep with you for the rest of the week and into tomorrow, when you start handling objections. Read it twice and pick one line to practice.
  • One clear reason beats ten features. More information does not create confidence; it creates work.
  • A.I.M. is MEDJAY's framework for structuring an offer, developed by Steven Saad: Anchor the pain in their words, Illuminate one benefit with checkable proof, Mobilize with one small next step.
  • Use A.I.M. in order, and only for presenting an offer.
  • Contrast is a choice: set it honestly using the customer's own numbers.
  • Small, useful next steps work because people follow through on what they have already started.
  • A real no is fine. Mobilize is an invitation, not a trap.

Note Keep tomorrow in mind: objections get their own method on Day 8, so do not improvise counters today.

Quiz

Answer the five questions below without looking back. Then check the answer key and the one-line explanation for each.

  1. What is the correct order of A.I.M.?

    1. Illuminate, Anchor, Mobilize
    2. Anchor, Illuminate, Mobilize
    3. Mobilize, Anchor, Illuminate
    4. Anchor, Mobilize, Illuminate

  2. A customer says ten features are easy to forget and hard to act on. What does this tell you about presenting?

    1. Add more detail so nothing is missed
    2. Lower the price first
    3. Lead with one clear reason tied to their problem
    4. Send everything in writing instead

  3. What counts as proof in the Illuminate step?

    1. A confident statement about your track record
    2. Something the customer can check for themselves, such as a test, demonstration or sample
    3. A long explanation of how your product is built
    4. A comparison with a competitor's weakness

  4. Why does Mobilize ask for a small next step rather than the whole decision?

    1. Because small requests are easier to refuse politely
    2. Because it hides the real price
    3. Because people tend to follow through on what they have already started, and a small useful step is easy to say yes to
    4. Because it delays the payment

  5. A B2B customer runs three sites. Which is the strongest Mobilize step?

    1. Sign the annual contract today
    2. Let me set up one site this week so you can watch it work
    3. Think it over and call me when you are ready
    4. I will email the full feature list

Upskill Challenge

Before the next day, use A.I.M. with three real customers or prospects in your own business. One conversation is fine. Write each one up straight afterward, while it is fresh.

Note The weakest step is the useful one. That is where your next practice session should go. - Use A.I.M. with three real people this week, at least one B2B and one B2C if your business serves both. - For each, write the Anchor in the customer's exact words, the one benefit you illuminated, the proof you offered, and the next step you requested. - In every B2B case, make the Mobilize step a paid pilot: scope, success criteria, duration and the path to a full deal, with a price attached. - Note which of the three steps felt weakest, and what happened afterward. - Bring one example where the customer said no to the next step. Write what you would change in your Mobilize question, not what you would say to push harder.

Model Answers

These are examples, not scripts. Compare the pattern to your own answer and keep whatever sounds like you.

Quiz answers:

  • 1. B. A.I.M. runs Anchor, Illuminate, Mobilize, in that order.
  • 2. C. People act on the one point that connects to their problem, so lead with one clear reason.
  • 3. B. Checkable proof beats claims; the customer must be able to verify it.
  • 4. C. Small commitments work through consistency, and the step must be easy and useful.
  • 5. B. One site is small, easy and useful; a signature is too big, and a feature list is the rookie mistake.

Note If your version is longer than the models, cut it. Length is the most common sign that the Anchor is weak. - Two-sentence B2B Anchor and Illuminate: 'You said your team loses two days every month building that report by hand. The setup pulls the same numbers into one report automatically, and I can run it once with your own spreadsheet so you see the two days come back.' Anchor uses her words; Illuminate gives one benefit plus proof she can check. - B2B Mobilize as a paid pilot: 'Would it work to run a paid pilot for one month on this one report, at a set fee and with two success criteria we write down now, and if it works we roll it out to the whole team?' It is a real commitment, it is bounded, and it protects both sides. - B2C Mobilize for the garden: 'Would it help if I came by for twenty minutes and walked the garden with you, so you can see what a first clean-up would involve before you commit to anything?' It is small, easy and useful even if he never books. - Answer to 'what do I get for that price?': 'You get the thing we talked about: no more jobs slipping through the spreadsheet. The price covers the setup that stops it. Do you want me to show you that on your own data first?' It returns to the Anchor instead of listing features. - Full A.I.M. offer in a service business: 'You told me the paperwork is eating your evenings. I take the whole admin side off your desk, and I can show you two current customers who stopped doing it themselves. Can we start with one week so you can see the evenings come back?' One problem, one benefit, checkable proof, one small step.
